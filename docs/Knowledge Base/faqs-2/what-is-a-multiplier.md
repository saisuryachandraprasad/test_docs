A multiplier measures how changes in one industry affect other industries within a regional economy. For example, if an industry has a multiplier of 2.5, then each positive or negative change in that industry generates a total impact 2.5 times greater across the region.

Lightcast develops its multipliers in house using a proprietary Input-Output model informed by

* Lightcast’s final unsuppressed industry data
* Gravitational flows
* Commuting patterns
* BEA make and use tables
* Additional regional and national data sources

## Sales Multipliers

A sales multiplier indicates how strongly an industry is embedded in the regional economy. Industries with high sales multipliers have extensive local supply chains and retain more economic value within the region. For example, a well-developed Automotive Manufacturing cluster may have a multiplier of 2.8 meaning every $1.00 of external sales generates $2.80 in total regional sales due to ripple effects.

Industries with low sales multipliers typically Are owned by entities outside the region (so profits leave immediately), and Purchase most of their inputs from outside the region

These characteristics create a **shallow root system**, limiting their broader economic impact.

## Jobs Multipliers

A jobs multiplier reflects how employment changes in one industry affect job creation in others. A multiplier of 3 means that for every job created in the initial industry, two additional jobs are created in the wider economy.

**Interpreting Jobs Multipliers**

Jobs multipliers can be misunderstood. Values of 17 or higher do occur, but they do not necessarily imply that attracting those industries is the most practical or effective strategy for regional growth. Jobs multipliers depend heavily on industry characteristics especially the sales-to-labor ratio

* Industries with high sales relative to labor tend to have high job multipliers.
* Industries with lower sales-to-labor ratios tend to have lower multipliers.

*Example*

A nuclear power plant may employ only 20 workers, yet requires millions of dollars in equipment and generates significant sales. Bringing 20 additional nuclear jobs into a region would require major capital investment and generate substantial economic activity including construction, professional services, and other support roles.

While this could produce a jobs multiplier of 15 (each nuclear job supporting 14 additional jobs), the cost and complexity of attracting such an industry are far greater than attracting 20 jobs in industries with lower multipliers.

## Earnings Multipliers

An earnings multiplier describes how much total labor income is generated throughout the economy for each dollar of new earnings in a scenario. For example, an earnings multiplier of 1.5 means every $1.00 of new earnings results in $1.50 in wages, salaries, and other compensation across the region.

Earnings multipliers help evaluate not just the number of jobs created, but the quality and income impact of those jobs.

*Example*

A scenario that indirectly brings lawyers and accountants into a region will generate significantly higher earnings than one that brings the same number of jobs in Food Services or Hospitality. As a result, the earnings multiplier for the first scenario would be much higher.
